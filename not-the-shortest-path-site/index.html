Introducing uncertainty into algorithmic navigation.

What happens when navigation is designed to encourage curiosity, attention, embodied perception, and encounters with the unexpected?

00

Background

We increasingly rely on algorithms to decide where we go, what we consume, and what deserves our attention.

The “best” answer is already selected.

Everyday decisions are increasingly shaped by systems that rank, filter, and recommend options for us.

Navigation

The Shortest Route

We use navigation systems to calculate the fastest and shortest way to move through the city.

Ratings

The Highest-Rated Restaurant

We use ratings and reviews to filter restaurants and identify what appears to be the best available choice.

Recommendation can narrow possibility.

When highly ranked choices become visible to everyone, different people can begin to follow the same paths.

Travel

The Same Tourist, Again

While traveling, you may repeatedly encounter the same person because you are both following the same algorithmically promoted travel guide post on social media.

Consumption

Highly Rated Restaurant, Not Necessarily Satisfying

You may wait in a long line for a highly rated restaurant, only to discover that the food does not suit your personal taste.

Algorithms Can Fail — But Our Tolerance for Error Is Shrinking.

Algorithms can be inaccurate or fail to anticipate unexpected conditions. Yet as we become accustomed to precise predictions and continuous updates, delays and disruptions can quickly lead to frustration, anxiety, and conflict.

Delivery

One Minute Late Delivery

When a delivery is only one minute late, we may begin refreshing the tracking page repeatedly because the system is no longer matching its predicted arrival time.

Mobility

Subway Delay

A subway delay can quickly produce frustration because the trip no longer follows the precise schedule shown by the navigation system.

Unexpected Conditions

Empty Shelves During Pandemic

During the pandemic, sudden changes in demand exceeded what inventory systems had predicted. Empty shelves were followed by arguments and blame online as people struggled with a situation that could no longer be controlled as expected.

What Changes?

Calculated certainty becomes a habit.

We become accustomed to accepting the supposedly best answer selected by an algorithm rather than responding to our own judgment and feelings. Although we appear constantly connected, we may engage less with our full senses and with the environments immediately around us.

We increasingly expect life to be precise, predictable, and controllable, as though data could eliminate uncertainty. Yet algorithms are not neutral or infallible. They are shaped by human classifications, assumptions, historical data, and design decisions.

Depending on calculated and seemingly guaranteed outcomes can gradually weaken our agency, sensory attention, and ability to respond to the unexpected.

Rhetorical Argument

“Navigation technologies do not simply describe the shortest path, they should help us to experience the space.”
The Default Fastest. Shortest. Predictable.
The Proposal Curious. Legible. Open to surprise.

Efficiency should be an available mode, not the unquestioned default for every journey.

09

Potential Capstone Project

A web-based prototype for generating destinations through numbers, color, and embodied interaction.
Possible Final Form

A navigation website where three user-generated numbers become an RGB color, the color reveals places, and the user constructs the route.

Instead of choosing destinations through distance, popularity, ratings, or an automatically calculated “best” result, the prototype uses color as an alternative relationship between the user and the city.

Three numbers create a color. The color reveals places. The user makes the route.
01

Generate Three Numbers

The user completes three rounds of interaction. Each round produces one number between 0 and 255.

02

Build an RGB Color

The first value becomes red, the second becomes green, and the third becomes blue.

03

Reveal Places

The resulting color reveals storefronts, signs, entrances, and building facades with related colors.

04

Construct the Route

The user selects the places that catch their attention, decides their order, and connects them into a route.

Three Input Rounds

Each action contributes one part of the color.

The user can choose a different interaction method for every round. This allows the final color to be partly intentional, partly bodily, and partly unpredictable.

User Agency

The user decides what becomes a route.

The system provides possible locations, but it does not decide which ones are important or how they should be connected.

01 Select places
02 Remove places
03 Choose the order
04 Add another place
05 Connect the route

Prototype Scope

A focused pilot rather than a complete citywide system.

01

Selected Pilot Area

The first prototype would focus on one defined area rather than attempting to map all of New York City.

02

Curated Place Dataset

Approximately 30–50 publicly accessible storefronts, signs, entrances, artworks, or building facades.

03

Color-Based Records

Each location includes an image, dominant color, name, address, category, and geographic coordinates.

04

User-Defined Route Logic

The user chooses the stops and their order. The map can connect those choices through walkable streets without selecting the destinations for them.
